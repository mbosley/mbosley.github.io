Using LLMs to Evaluate the Evidence Support for Grand Theories of Politics at Scale

Tore Wig and Mitchell Bosley

Methodological Overview: The Iterative Synthesis Cycle

1. Theory Deconstruction & Implication Generation
2. Running Literature Review (RLR) Initialization
Iterate for each paper in corpus:
3a. LLM: Analyze Paper & Update RLR
3b. LLM: Elicit Likelihoods
3c. Calculate Implication Posterior
4. Aggregate Outputs: Final RLR, Belief Trajectories
5. Update Confidence in Grand Theory

Phase 1: Foundation & Framework Setup

1. Deconstructing Theory: LLM-Assisted Implication Generation

The LLM analyzes seminal texts of a grand theory to extract its core, empirically falsifiable implications (I1, I2, …, In).

LLM Prompt: Implication Generation

Given Fearon's (1995) "Rationalist Explanations for War" [Core Excerpts Provided]:

Identify 3-5 central, distinct, and empirically testable implications stemming from the theory's mechanisms (private information, commitment problems, issue indivisibility).

For each implication, provide:
  a. A clear statement.
  b. Its theoretical linkage.
  c. Examples of relevant empirical evidence.

LLM Output: Example Implication (I1)

Implication 1: Impact of Private Information on Conflict Risk
  a. Statement: Conflict likelihood increases with significant private information on capabilities/resolve, especially with incentives to misrepresent.
  b. Linkage: Directly from Fearon's argument on informational asymmetries obscuring the bargaining range.
  c. Evidence Examples: Studies on crisis escalation and informational opacity; signaling effectiveness; experimental bargaining under uncertainty.

2. Initializing the Analytical Structure

A Running Literature Review (RLR) document is created with sections for each implication Ij. Each Ij is assigned an initial numerical prior probability, P(Ij)initial, typically 0.5 (maximal uncertainty).

Example: Setup for Implication I1

Initial RLR Section for I1:

SECTION: Implication 1 - Impact of Private Information on Conflict Risk

DEFINITION:
Conflict likelihood increases with significant private information held by at least one party regarding their military capabilities or resolve, coupled with incentives to misrepresent that information during bargaining. This directly stems from Fearon's argument that informational asymmetries prevent states from identifying mutually preferable negotiated settlements that lie within the bargaining range.

THEORETICAL CONTEXT:
This implication is a cornerstone of rationalist explanations for war. It posits that what actors don't know (and cannot credibly learn) about each other's private information can be a direct cause of costly conflict, even when a peaceful settlement would be mutually preferable ex-post.

INITIAL ASSESSMENT (PRE-LITERATURE REVIEW):
The mechanism has strong theoretical coherence and face validity. The degree of empirical support across various contexts and its relative importance compared to other causal factors (e.g., commitment problems) is to be determined through the subsequent literature review. No specific evidence has been processed yet for this implication.

CURRENT NUMERICAL BELIEF: P(I1)initial = 0.5

Initial Numerical Prior for I1: P(I1)initial = 0.5

Phase 2: Iterative Evidence Synthesis (Processing Schultz 1999 for I1)

A. LLM Evidence Extraction & Qualitative RLR Update (The Reasoning Trace)

The LLM reads Schultz (1999), identifies relevant evidence for I1, and integrates it into the RLR.

LLM Prompt: RLR Update & Evidence Assessment

Current RLR for Implication I1:
[Insert full current RLR text for I1 from previous step]

Paper for Analysis: Schultz (1999) "Do Democratic Institutions Constrain or Inform?"
[Detailed Abstract Provided: Schultz (1999) examines whether democratic institutions primarily constrain leaders from using force or provide information that clarifies their intentions and capabilities. Using statistical analysis of militarized interstate disputes, he finds that democracies are not necessarily more peaceful overall but are more selective in their engagements. The transparency of democratic processes, including public debate and opposition scrutiny, makes it more costly for democratic leaders to bluff. Consequently, threats made by democracies are perceived as more credible, leading to opponents being more likely to concede when a democracy issues a serious challenge. This suggests democracies are better at signaling their true resolve due to reduced informational asymmetries.]

Instructions:
1. Is Schultz (1999) relevant to Implication I1? Justify.
2. Synthesize Schultz's core argument and findings related to Implication I1.
3. Evaluate how this evidence impacts Implication I1 (support, refute, refine). Consider evidence strength (methodology, scope, clarity).
4. Compose an updated RLR section for Implication I1, integrating Schultz's contribution into the existing narrative. This text must serve as a comprehensive qualitative justification for belief changes. Ensure clear attribution to Schultz (1999).

LLM Output: Updated RLR Section for I1

SECTION: Implication 1 - Impact of Private Information on Conflict Risk

... [Previous RLR Text for I1, including Definition, Theoretical Context, Initial Assessment] ...

EVIDENCE UPDATE (Schultz 1999):
The initial theoretical plausibility of private information acting as a significant cause of war finds empirical grounding when considering how different institutional structures affect information transmission during crises.

Kenneth Schultz's (1999) study, "Do Democratic Institutions Constrain or Inform?", offers compelling evidence for this. Schultz argues that democratic systems, characterized by public deliberation and opposition oversight, inherently increase the transparency of a state's intentions and resolve. This transparency makes it more costly and difficult for democratic leaders to bluff or misrepresent their private information about their willingness to use force.

His empirical analysis of militarized interstate disputes indicates that threats from democracies are generally perceived as more credible by adversaries. Consequently, opponents are more likely to concede when faced with a serious challenge from a democracy, not because democracies are inherently more pacific, but because their signals regarding resolve are clearer and less prone to misinterpretation due to reduced informational asymmetry.

This research directly underpins Implication 1. If bargaining failures often stem from an inability to overcome private information problems (specifically, discerning true resolve amidst incentives to bluff), then institutional mechanisms like those in democracies that enhance signal credibility should lead to fewer conflicts triggered by miscalculation. Schultz's work effectively demonstrates such a pathway.

The study's use of quantitative analysis across a broad set of interstate disputes provides robust, moderate-to-strong support for the idea that mitigating private information about resolve can indeed reduce the likelihood of bargaining breakdown. This evidence substantially strengthens our confidence in the empirical validity of Implication 1.

ASSESSMENT OF SCHULTZ (1999) FOR I1: Supporting. Strength: Moderate to Strong.

B. LLM-Informed Likelihood Elicitation (Quantifying RLR Insights)

The LLM translates its qualitative RLR assessment into numerical likelihoods.

LLM Prompt: Likelihood Estimation

Context: Your RLR update for I1 based on Schultz (1999) states: "Schultz's work effectively demonstrates such a pathway... provides robust, moderate-to-strong support... strengthening confidence in Implication I1."

Task: Based on your detailed RLR assessment of Schultz (1999) for Implication I1:
1.  P(Schultz's Evidence | I1 is True): Estimate the probability (0.0-1.0) of observing Schultz's specific findings IF Implication I1 IS fundamentally TRUE.
2.  P(Schultz's Evidence | I1 is False): Estimate the probability (0.0-1.0) of observing Schultz's findings IF Implication I1 IS fundamentally FALSE.
3.  Justification: Provide a concise rationale for your probability estimates, explicitly linking them to the strengths and relevance of Schultz's evidence as documented in your RLR update.

LLM Output: Likelihood Estimates & Justification

1. P(Schultz's Evidence | I1 is True): 0.80
2. P(Schultz's Evidence | I1 is False): 0.25
3. Justification:
The RLR update emphasized that Schultz (1999) provides 'robust, moderate-to-strong support' by demonstrating a clear 'pathway' through which private information about resolve is mitigated by democratic institutions, aligning directly with Implication I1.
If I1 is TRUE, it is highly probable (thus 0.80) that we would find empirical studies like Schultz's that identify specific institutional factors mitigating these informational problems and demonstrating their impact on crisis bargaining outcomes.
If I1 is FALSE (i.e., private information is not a major driver of war), it would be considerably less probable (thus 0.25) to find such a specific and empirically supported link. While democratic behavior might be studied for other reasons, its direct tie-in as a solution to a non-existent private information problem would be less likely.

C. Formal Bayesian Update (Calculating Belief Revision for I1)

Belief Update Calculation for I1

Using Bayes' Theorem: P(H|E) = [P(E|H) * P(H)] / P(E)

For Implication I1 and Schultz's Evidence (ES):
P(I1 True | ES) = [P(ES | I1 True) * P(I1 True)initial] / P(ES)
where P(ES) = [P(ES | I1 True) * P(I1 True)initial] + [P(ES | I1 False) * P(I1 False)initial]

Given: P(I1 True)initial = 0.5; P(ES | I1 True) = 0.80; P(ES | I1 False) = 0.25

Calculation: Numerator = 0.80 * 0.5 = 0.40. Denominator = (0.80 * 0.5) + (0.25 * 0.5) = 0.40 + 0.125 = 0.525.

Posterior P(I1 True | ES) = 0.40 / 0.525 ≈ 0.762

Confidence in I1 increased from 0.5 to approx. 0.762.

2. Updating Confidence in the Grand Theory

The overall confidence in the grand theory (GT) is updated based on the posterior probabilities of its constituent implications.

Theory-Level Belief Update

Conceptual Approach: If a grand theory GT implies I1, I2, …, In, then the support for GT depends on the collective support for these implications.

  1. Simple Aggregation (Illustrative): A weighted average of the final posterior probabilities of the implications.
    Example: P(GT) ≈ Σ [wj * P(Ij)final], where Σwj = 1.
  2. Logical Conjunction (Illustrative): If GT critically depends on ALL implications being true:
    P(GT | Eall) is proportional to Π P(Ij | Ej) * P(GT)initial.
  3. Advanced Models (e.g., Item Response Theory - IRT): Model implications as "test items" measuring an underlying latent trait (the "truth" or "validity" of the grand theory). Each paper's finding for an implication acts as a "response." IRT can estimate the difficulty/informativeness of each implication and provide a more nuanced measure of the theory's overall standing. This approach can also help identify which implications are most central or discriminative for the theory.
  4. Qualitative Synthesis by LLM (Recommended for Richness): The LLM reviews final RLRs and belief states for all implications, providing an overall qualitative assessment and a synthesized numerical confidence score for GT.

Example (using Qualitative Synthesis by LLM):

LLM Output: Overall Grand Theory Assessment

Considering the final evidential support for Fearon's Bargaining Theory implications:
- Implication I1 (Private Info): Robust support (P approx. 0.85 final). The RLR shows consistent findings across different methodologies, though some recent work points to moderating factors in the modern information environment.
- Implication I2 (Commitment): Moderate support (P approx. 0.65 final). The RLR indicates strong support in interstate contexts but more mixed or contested evidence regarding civil war terminations.
- Implication I3 (Indivisibility): Weaker, more context-dependent support (P approx. 0.40 final). The RLR suggests this mechanism is invoked less frequently and often relies on specific case interpretations rather than broad empirical tests.

Overall, Fearon's Bargaining Theory receives substantial, though not uniform, empirical backing. Its private information mechanism is well-corroborated as a significant driver of conflict. The commitment problem logic is generally supported but faces scope conditions and domain-specific challenges. Issue indivisibility remains a less empirically validated and potentially more niche component of the theory.

Synthesized confidence in the overall theory, given this pattern of support and the centrality of the core mechanisms: Moderate to Strong (e.g., a synthesized score of 0.70, reflecting strong performance on its most central claims but weaknesses in others). Further research should focus on the conditional factors affecting commitment problems and developing more robust tests for issue indivisibility.
