Relevant literature

The prior work establishes two mechanisms and one unresolved measurement problem.

  1. 1

    Social presence changes honesty and disclosure.

    Cohn, Gesche & Maréchal (2022) find substantially more financially motivated cheating during machine interaction than human interaction, consistent with social-image concerns. Schuetzler et al. (2018) show that conversational agents, face-to-face interviews, and online surveys elicit different responses to sensitive questions—and that more humanlike conversation can increase socially desirable responding. Together, these studies make the perceived social audience a central mechanism.

  2. 2

    Admission rates require behavioral validation.

    Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi (2013) establish the incentivized die-roll paradigm, while Höglinger & Jann (2018) add an individually recorded roll. Their validation shows why both false denials and false admissions matter: a method can produce more sensitive admissions while also creating more incorrect ones.

  3. 3

    Validation requires an independent truth benchmark.

    Rosenfeld, Imai & Shapiro (2016) demonstrate that sensitive-question methods should be evaluated against known outcomes. Höglinger and Jann extend that principle to the individual level, where truthful admissions, false denials, truthful denials, and false admissions can be separated directly.

The open gap

Prior studies contribute separate pieces: the social audience during behavior, the mode used for sensitive disclosure, and validation against known outcomes. This experiment brings those pieces together. It records behavior first and then randomizes human, AI, or self-administered elicitation, isolating the effect of interviewer mode on truthful disclosure.

Theoretical framework, hypotheses, and analysis

Theoretical framework

Admitting dishonest behavior can feel socially costly. A human interviewer may heighten concern about judgment. A standard survey removes that interpersonal audience. An AI interviewer may combine the privacy of self-administration with the engagement of a conversation, making truthful admission easier.

Primary · H1

More people who misreported will admit it to an AI interviewer than to a human interviewer.

This is the main comparison.

Secondary · H2

More people who misreported will admit it to an AI interviewer than on a standard survey.

This tests whether an AI conversation adds value beyond private self-administration.

Secondary · H3

More people who misreported will admit it on a standard survey than to a human interviewer.

This tests whether human social presence discourages truthful admission.

Primary analysis Among verified misreporters: who admits it?

Compare the percentage who truthfully admit misreporting across the three arms. AI versus human is the primary test.

Accuracy safeguard Among verified honest reporters: who says they misreported?

Compare false admissions across the three arms. This distinguishes truthful disclosure from a general increase in “yes” responses.

What can we learn with 600 participants?

With 200 participants in each arm, the study is powered to detect a large difference between AI and human interviewers. If approximately 30% of participants misreport during the behavioral task, AI would need to increase truthful admissions from about 25% to 50% for us to detect the effect reliably.

Core contribution

By recording behavior before randomizing the elicitation mode, the study isolates whether human, AI, or self-administered interviews most effectively elicit truthful disclosure.
